## Stage 1 — item 1, gap fills. **DONE.** `results/nnfx/GAP_FILLS.md`

**The one number: a long EURCHF into the de-peg loses −1.000 R on the engine's
books and up to −20.100 R in reality, and the leverage cap scales none of it.**

Three lines:

1. **One of the three fill cases was wrong and it is the one that matters.**

2. **Fixed, standing, and guarded.** `l2engine._gap_px` fills at the first
   tradable price at or beyond the stop, on the side that would actually fill;
   pre-flight guard 41 refuses a Layer 2 table without it; the hourly-OFF path
   is byte-identical to the old engine (SHA `e5264776bbacc833` both ways).
3. **It changes no verdict on A, B or C** — the largest move to a candidate
   total over six years is 2.3 R, and 1 of 9,153 entries loses more than 2 R.
   **That is a fact about this sample, not about the exposure:** the book was

### The measurement that matters

a LONG EURCHF held into 15 January 2015, at the engine's own sizing.

| stop | what the engine booked | the hourly rule | the bound |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1.5 ATR (16.5 pips) | **−1.00 R** | −1.00 R | **−20.10 R** |
| 31 ATR | −20.67 R | **−99.95 R** | −101.44 R |

Three lines on that:

1. **At a realistic stop the hourly path cannot prove the gap, and the honest
   answer is a bound, not a number.** The de-peg happened inside one hourly bar
   (09:00 UTC: bid open 1.20083, low 1.16777) whose range covers any stop near
   the peg — so hour resolution says "price traded through it" and fills at the
   stop. The next hour opened at **1.03592**. What a tick path would have filled
   is somewhere between the stop and 1.16777: **up to 20 times the intended
   risk**, and the engine books 1.0 R.
2. **Where the gap IS resolvable the correction is enormous.** A stop below
   1.16777 breaches in the 10:00 bar instead, and the first tradable price is
   that bar's open, 1.03592. A −20.67 R loss becomes **−99.95 R**.
3. **The leverage cap is no protection against this.** That position is
   **7.3:1** — comfortably inside the 1:30 prop limit — so the cap scales it by
   1.000 and capped size equals engine size. The cap limits how much you can
   hold, not what a hole in the market does to what you are holding.

## Stage 2 — item 2, the quiet-entry test. **DONE.** `results/nnfx/QUIET_ENTRY.md`

**The one number: the walk-forward quiet-entry rule loses −4.816 R on A and
−25.689 R on B against its own control, 1 of 6 years positive in both. Do not
adopt it.**

Three lines:

1. **The rule fails two of the three conditions set before the run.** It does
   not beat its own control on the paired test (0 of 2 candidates), and the
   fixed-Q curve turns direction 4 times for A and 5 times for B out of 8
   steps — a maximum in noise, not a plateau. With hindsight A at Q = 0.40
   makes +44.0 R against +39.9 R unfiltered and Q = 0.20 doubles return per
   dip; picked the only way you actually could, it loses. **This is the
   cleanest hindsight-versus-walk-forward demonstration in the project.**
2. **The thing underneath it is real, and it is the other way round.** 35 of
   39 C1 alone show R per trade falling as the ATR decile rises, and all 39
   lose money overall — so it is **losses concentrated in loud entries**, not
   profit in quiet ones. The decile-matched null confirms it is in the
   entries, not the axis: real entries in the top deciles underperform random
   entries drawn from the *same* decile, same holding period, same direction
   mix, by up to one standard deviation. **Candidate A — the one the
   hypothesis came from — is the flattest thing in the table** (rho +0.055).
3. **And the floor and the quiet rule do conflict — the floor is the one that
   loses.** With the quiet rule off, turning the 5% per-pair floor OFF is
   worth **+12.553 R and 30% of return per dip on A**, and **+6.008 R and 37%
   on B**, and floor-off wins at 3 of 4 and 2 of 4 Q values. That is a result
   about a rule you already decided; it is recorded and **not changed**.

**Flagged, not built:** the surviving asymmetry is about SIZE, not admission —
a top-decile entry is a worse bet and carries more notional for the same R.
Scaling risk down by ATR decile would act on the measured effect without
throwing away three quarters of the sample, which is what kills the Q cut. That
is a different experiment and was not in item 2.

## Stage 3 — item 3, the per-pair bank and pair groups. **DONE.**
`results/nnfx/PER_PAIR_BANK.md`, `results/nnfx/PAIR_GROUPS.md`

**The one number: picking the best strategy per group on four tuning years
makes −50.96 R pooled and −94.04 R on the Layer 1 character grouping, against a
hindsight ceiling of +520.64 R. The dispersion is real. No tested rule reaches
any of it.**

Three lines:

1. **The bank is built the way the new direction needs: 38,919 cells**, 47
   candidates × 28 pairs × 2 directions × every year 2005–2020, each year
   labelled tune / unseen / sealed. The leverage cap is applied over the whole
   28-pair frame and *then* split, so a group's R is its share of a book that
   was achievable as a whole. **Only 4 of 47 candidates are positive on the
   unseen block** (A +41.49, B +29.59, waddah_attar +19.63, laguerre +12.42),
   and **A is the only broadly based one** — 33 of 56 cells positive, best cell
   18.0% of its total. `glitch_index` has one cell worth 12.9× its own total.
2. **The assignment step destroys value.** Every grouping is worse than
   pooling, and the Layer 1 pair-character grouping is **worse than its own
   shuffled labels** — null p 0.987, z −2.27, 987 of 1,000 random regroupings
   beat it. The reason is measurable: **pair character does not persist.**
   Trendiness before 2010 correlates **−0.456** with 2010–2015 and only 21.4%
   of pairs keep their label. Cost per ATR does persist (+0.919, 64.3%).
3. **The diversification the book needs IS available; the selection is not.**
   Within a grouping the sleeves are near-independent (groups correlate 0.00 to
   0.26 on daily P&L) and A against B is 0.370. So combining sleeves will work
   as arithmetic. Choosing which sleeve goes where, by recent performance, does
   not work at all.

**This does not say the book plan is wrong.** It says an assignment cannot be
made by ranking candidates on recent performance — the only mechanism tested.
An assignment from something that is not performance was the alternative, and
pair character was the obvious candidate for it; 3b has just shown it does not
persist.

## Stage 4 — item 4, declared mean reversion. **DONE.**
`results/nnfx/MEAN_REVERSION.md`

**The one number: every mean-reversion variant is NEGATIVELY correlated with
candidate A, −0.124 to −0.397. That is the only thing here that is not
borderline.**

Three lines:

1. **Not proven on its own, and the condition is doing all the work.** 7 of 24
   cells are positive and **not one reaches |t| = 2**; every cell that does
   reach |t| ≥ 2 is negative. The best is `MR120` conditional on all 28 pairs:
   **+18.507 R over six years, +3.084 a year**, t 0.602, null p 0.110, 3 of 6
   years positive. Unconditional, the same rule makes **−83.542 R** with a
   222.5 R dip. The filter is the difference between a disaster and an
   unproven candidate.
2. **Two corrections to the brief, both material.** (a) The early work's
   condition was the **9-box** (`HANDOFF_3.md` §10), not a half-life or a
   stationarity test — the repo had a Hurst exponent and neither of the other
   two, so both were built new. (b) **The half-life is not binding**: it
   passes on 96.7–99.9% of bars while the Dickey-Fuller test passes on 9.3%
   (MR120), 18.0% (MR60) and 90.4% (MR20). "Both agree" *is* the ADF test
   alone.
3. **Flagged, and it is the reason not to like it: crisis dependence.** Every
   positive cell is over the 10% standing threshold and `MR120` takes **73.6%**
   of its R from crisis windows. A mean-reversion rule that earns three
   quarters of its money during crises is betting the biggest stretches come
   back — and the one event this project has studied in detail, the SNB
   de-peg, is a stretch that did not.

Also: the Layer 1 `RANGING` gate helps in **1 of 6** conditional comparisons
(and it is in-sample over these years anyway), and "mean reversion for
range-prone pairs" is **not** supported — range-prone wins on R per trade in 5
of 12 and on R per dip in 6 of 12, a coin flip, exactly as item 3b's
non-persistence predicts.

## Stage 6c — drawdown control. **DONE.** `results/nnfx/DRAWDOWN.md`

**The one number: the best of 25 swept control settings improves return per dip
by 16.5%, and the curve is not monotone.**

Three lines:

1. **A drawdown control cannot add return**, only let the same return be taken
   at a larger size. The best single cell is `equity_pause` at 25% of the dip
   for 10 days: return per dip 1.322 against 1.135. It is the **maximum over 25
   cells on one six-year sample** and the curve turns over as the one knob
   moves by one notch (no change at 5 days, best at 10, worse than baseline at
   20) — so an upper bound on what is there, not a setting to adopt.
2. **A kill switch on this book is a way of losing the book.** At 50% of the
   binding dip it turns +39.874 R into −13.348 R; the higher levels never fire
   at all, because they are fractions of DIP95 (35.125 R) while the realised
   drawdown is 22.616 R. **Crisis sizing costs money monotonically** — zeroing
   size in crisis windows costs 26.4% of the total and the dip barely moves,
   because A's worst drawdown is not in a crisis window.
3. **A fault found and fixed in the first run, because it looked like a
   result:** `equity_pause` measured drawdown from the peak of the *managed*
   equity, so while paused it could never recover and re-triggered forever — a
   pause that cannot end is a kill switch. It showed up as byte-identical
   numbers at 5, 10 and 20 days. The peak now resets on resumption.
