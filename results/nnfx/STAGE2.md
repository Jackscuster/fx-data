## One of the four baseline rules cannot be switched off, and the engine is why

Jack asked which rule inside the baseline does the work, by switching each on alone. Three can be. The fourth cannot.


`side` is the sign of (close − baseline), recomputed every bar (`l2engine.py:274-279`). Every entry route then gates on agreement, and agreement contains `side`:


```
agree_long = c1_lc[i] and c2_lc[i] and v_ok_l[i] and side == 1   (:527)
r1l = use_base_cross and last_cross_bar == i and side == 1 and agree_long
r2l = use_c1_flip   and c1_lt[i] and agree_long                (:549-552)
```

There is no path into a trade that avoids it. So the **side filter is the floor**, and the other three are measured as INCREMENTS OVER IT, not over nothing. Reporting them as independent additions would credit whichever rule was switched on with the side filter's entire effect.

## 2b — the paired test: the same C1 with and without the baseline

Both series live through the same 2010–2015 markets, so differencing their daily P&L day by day removes everything common to them and leaves the baseline's own contribution. The block-bootstrap t is on that difference, 20-day blocks, 2,000 draws.


The days are **unioned, not intersected**. A day on which only one of the two is in the market is a real difference and belongs in the test; intersecting would delete the baseline's main effect, which is to stop trades happening. A missing day is zero P&L, not missing data.

## 2d — the cost cap

Pairs are ranked each year by round-trip cost divided by ATR. The cost is the era table's; the ATR is measured over the **previous** calendar year and never the year being traded, so the ranking is known in advance and the trade year stays unseen. No performance is used.


cost / ATR is the right ratio because R is measured in ATR units: a pair whose spread is a large share of its daily range eats a large share of every R, whatever its absolute pip cost.

### What 2d says, in plain English

No. A cost cap does not raise net R per year or return per dip on any candidate that was making money without it, with a single exception that does not survive a second look. Of the 9 candidate-settings on the main plan, 2 are profitable over the unseen years at the full 28-pair set; for 0 of those 2 the best cap on R per year IS the full set, and for 0 of 2 so is the best cap on return per dip. The median gain from the best cap across the profitable candidates is +1.615 R per year and +0.3155 on return per dip — zero to three decimals. Where the cap appears to help it is helping a LOSER: it improves every one of the 7 unprofitable settings, which is not an improvement but simply doing less of something that loses money.

The one exception is **halftrend at its default setting**, where capping to 15 pairs lifts R per year from +0.55 to +1.96 and capping to 11 pairs lifts return per dip from 0.0357 to 0.5622. I would not act on it: it is one cell in 9, the uncapped base it improves on is barely above zero, and **the two metrics choose different caps** (15 pairs against 11). A real cost effect would put both at the same place on the curve; two different answers from the same sweep is what noise looks like.


This is the same conclusion the first version of 2d reached, and it survives the engine fix — the dip changed, the ranking of caps did not. **Cheap pairs do not carry the edge**, even though the dearest pair costs three times the cheapest (EURCHF 0.070 against EURUSD 0.023 per unit of gross R, from `ENGINE_AUDIT_2.md`).


## 2e — fewer trades

A minimum gap between trades on a pair, and a minimum signal age, each swept over its full range. The gap is applied greedily forward in time: take a trade, then refuse every entry within the gap of that entry. ### What 2e says, in plain English

Yes, and only for ichimoku — but there it is the clearest effect in the whole staged build. A minimum gap between trades raises net R per year AND return per dip on **all three** of ichimoku's settings, and all three choose the same cut, a **34-day minimum gap**. That agreement across settings is the part worth something: a single lucky cut on one setting would be a coincidence, three settings landing on the same cut is a property of the indicator. At the default setting the gap takes total R from -9.07 to 4.77 on 77% of the trades, R per trade from -0.0126 to +0.0087, and return per dip from -0.0425 to 0.0314. So the answer to Jack's actual question — does net R per trade rise faster than the trade count falls — is YES on ichimoku: it keeps about three quarters of the trades and more than doubles R per trade, so total R goes UP rather than merely surviving.

It does not generalise. On **schaff_trend_cycle** the half setting improves at an 8-day cut and the default setting improves at NO cut in the whole sweep — its best is the uncapped base. On **halftrend** there is nothing: the largest gain anywhere is under one R over six years, and its half setting loses money at every cut. Across all 18 candidate-setting-rule combinations only 3 that were already profitable improve at all.


The honest reading is that **ichimoku holds its trades too briefly and benefits from being made to wait**, which is a statement about one indicator and not about the NNFX algorithm. Nothing here has been null-tested yet: the cut was swept over its full range and the best of the sweep is reported, so a selection correction is still owed before the 34-day gap is treated as a finding rather than a lead.
