## One of the four baseline rules cannot be switched off, and the engine is why

Jack asked which rule inside the baseline does the work, by switching each on alone. Three can be. The fourth cannot.


`side` is the sign of (close − baseline), recomputed every bar (`l2engine.py:274-279`). Every entry route then gates on agreement, and agreement contains `side`:


```
agree_long = c1_lc[i] and c2_lc[i] and v_ok_l[i] and side == 1   (:527)
r1l = use_base_cross and last_cross_bar == i and side == 1 and agree_long
r2l = use_c1_flip   and c1_lt[i] and agree_long                (:549-552)
```

There is no path into a trade that avoids it. So the **side filter is the floor**, and the other three are measured as INCREMENTS OVER IT, not over nothing. Reporting them as independent additions would credit whichever rule was switched on with the side filter's entire effect.

## 2b — the paired test: the same C1 with and without the baseline

Both series live through the same 2010–2015 markets, so differencing their daily P&L day by day removes everything common to them and leaves the baseline's own contribution. The block-bootstrap t is on that difference, 20-day blocks, 2,000 draws.


The days are **unioned, not intersected**. A day on which only one of the two is in the market is a real difference and belongs in the test; intersecting would delete the baseline's main effect, which is to stop trades happening. A missing day is zero P&L, not missing data.

## 2d — the cost cap

Pairs are ranked each year by round-trip cost divided by ATR. The cost is the era table's; the ATR is measured over the **previous** calendar year and never the year being traded, so the ranking is known in advance and the trade year stays unseen. No performance is used.


cost / ATR is the right ratio because R is measured in ATR units: a pair whose spread is a large share of its daily range eats a large share of every R, whatever its absolute pip cost.

## 2e — fewer trades

A minimum gap between trades on a pair, and a minimum signal age, each swept over its full range. The gap is applied greedily forward in time: take a trade, then refuse every entry within the gap of that entry. Greedy-forward is the only causal version — choosing which of two close trades to keep by their outcome would use the future.


Jack's question is whether net R per trade rises faster than trade count falls, so both are in the table and so is total R, because a rule that lifts R per trade while destroying total R is not an improvement.
